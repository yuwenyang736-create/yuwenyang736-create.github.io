Historical Background

In 1987, Taiwan lifted martial law and began reopening contact with mainland China. As cross-strait exchanges increased, marriage migration became increasingly common during the 1990s and early 2000s. Many mainland Chinese women moved to Taiwan after marrying Taiwanese men, hoping to build new lives and families. However, their lives were often shaped by restrictive immigration policies, unequal power within marriage, and social discrimination. Before obtaining permanent legal status, mainland spouses faced limitations on their rights to work and reside in Taiwan. For women experiencing domestic violence, dependence on their husbands could make leaving an abusive marriage especially difficult.[1][2]

One of these women was Zhao Yanbing (趙岩冰), a mainland Chinese immigrant who moved to Taiwan in 2003 after marrying a Taiwanese man. During their marriage, Zhao experienced repeated physical violence, verbal humiliation, and financial conflict. Although she twice obtained court protection orders against her husband, the abuse continued. On February 1, 2006, during a confrontation in which her husband threatened her with a kitchen knife, Zhao struck him with a hammer and subsequently stabbed him with the knife, causing his death. The case attracted public attention and raised difficult questions about domestic violence, self-defense, and the experiences of immigrant women in Taiwan.[3]

In September 2007, the Taipei District Court recognized Zhao's history of prolonged abuse and considered her actions within the framework of battered woman syndrome. The court acknowledged that she had acted defensively against an unlawful threat but concluded that her use of force was excessive. She received a three-year prison sentence, reduced to eighteen months under a separate sentence-reduction law. Prosecutors appealed, arguing in part that Zhao's education and her previous efforts to seek police protection did not fit their understanding of a typical abused woman. Women's rights advocates challenged this reasoning, questioning why a victim's willingness to resist or seek help should undermine recognition of her abuse.[3][4]

Zhao's case emerged during a period of significant legal change in Taiwan. In 1993, the widely publicized case of Deng Ruwen 鄧如雯, another woman who killed her abusive husband, had helped inspire a movement for domestic violence legislation. In 1998, Taiwan enacted its Domestic Violence Prevention Act, establishing a legal framework for protecting victims of family violence. Yet Zhao's experience in 2006 revealed the continuing limitations of legal protection. In March 2007, Taiwan revised the Act, removing the goal of "promoting family harmony" from its stated purpose to place greater emphasis on preventing abuse and protecting victims.[5][6]

Immigration law was also changing. In November 2007, following years of advocacy by women's and immigrant rights organizations, Taiwan passed amendments to its Immigration Act that provided certain foreign spouses experiencing domestic violence with opportunities to extend their residence after their marriage ended. These protections remained limited and did not automatically grant permanent residence. Mainland Chinese spouses were governed by a separate legal framework, which was further reformed in 2009 to expand their employment and residency rights. The distinction exposed how legal status, national origin, and marital relationships could determine a woman's ability to escape violence and rebuild her life.[2][7][8]

Similar tensions exist in the United States. The Violence Against Women Act (VAWA), enacted in 1994, established an important immigration protection for survivors of domestic abuse. Under VAWA, qualifying immigrant spouses, children, and parents of abusive U.S. citizens or lawful permanent residents can petition for immigration status independently, without relying on the participation or consent of their abusers. This provides a possible route toward lawful permanent residence and reduces the power an abusive family member can exercise through immigration sponsorship.[9]

However, these protections remain vulnerable to changes in immigration enforcement and policy. In January 2025, the Trump administration withdrew federal immigration enforcement restrictions at certain sensitive locations, prompting concerns that immigrant survivors might avoid shelters and other essential services out of fear of detention or deportation. In December 2025, new federal guidance also tightened aspects of the VAWA self-petition process. Although VAWA remains in effect, these developments illustrate the continuing tension between the promise of legal protection and the realities facing immigrant survivors.[10][11]

Inspired by Zhao Yanbing's story, Her Rage revisits the case through magical realism and the figure of Medea. Moving beyond the courtroom's account of a woman who killed her husband, the play explores the social and political forces surrounding her actions. It asks what happens when a woman is denied control over her own life, and when the law recognizes her suffering but cannot fully protect her. At its heart, Her Rage is not simply a story of violence. It is a story about immigration, gender, and power.
